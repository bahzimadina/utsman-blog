Six posts in one week, six topics that look unrelated at first: an activity report, presentation material, choosing a channel, scheduled automation, a report for donors, and a handover between committee members. Read alone, each one stands on its own. Read in order, they all point to the same habit.

## Start from the reader's chair

When you draft an activity report, the useful first question is not what we did, but who will read this and in what state. Usually a busy person, maybe on the way somewhere, maybe with two minutes before the next meeting. Once that picture is clear, one page stops feeling thin and starts feeling right.

Presentation material works the same way. The number of slides decides very little; one message that has to stay in the listener's head decides the rest. An hour is still enough when the message is chosen first and the app is opened later.

Channel choice follows the same logic. WhatsApp for things that need to be read quickly, Telegram for files and conversations worth keeping, email for matters that need a formal trail. The question is not which one is fanciest, but where the reader actually is.

## Readers who are not in the room yet

Scheduled automation sounds technical. But its reader is a person too: you three months from now, or the committee member who takes over. A routine that runs by itself every morning is really a note written for someone who later will not have to remember anything.

The donor report is the clearest version of all this. A donor is not a boss grading diligence. They are someone who already trusted us and wants to know whether that trust turned into something. That is why a good report starts with what changed, not with the committee list.

The handover closes the week with the same lesson. Checklists, links, and short notes sound trivial until you picture a new person opening that folder with no context at all. For them, a one-line note can save a whole programme.

## Questions that came up this week

**If one page is enough, what gets cut?** Not the data, but the ceremonial layer: greetings, committee lists, attendance sheets. Numbers and evidence stay. The rest moves into an appendix for anyone who wants to dig.

**Does automation not push us further from the work?** It does when it is installed and then forgotten. A healthy setup still has a human reading the output. The schedule runs itself; the decision stays with a person.

**What if there are many readers with different needs?** Write for one primary reader, then provide an appendix for the rest. A single version for everyone usually ends up fitting nobody.

Six posts, one thread: write and arrange the work with the reader in mind. Not so it looks tidy, but so the work actually arrives. 🧵
