Meeting invitations, requests, circulars, reports, letters to outside agencies. They pass across a committee desk almost every day. One badly written letter can push a matter back, or make the organization look like it is not taking things seriously.

So writing a formal letter should be a habit, not a memorized format.

## Know Who Is Reading

Before you write a single sentence, ask two things. Who will read this, and what do we want to happen afterward?

A letter to the head of an agency sounds different from a letter to a resident. One needs standard, orderly language, the other can be warmer. Either way, the purpose must show up in the first paragraph. If the reader has to guess what you want by the third paragraph, the letter has half failed already.

## The Parts People Treat Lightly

Letter number, date, attachments, subject line, and the recipient's name. These five get filled in quickly, yet this is where wrong titles and wrong places show up most.

One habit helps: fill them in last, after the body is finished. Once the content is settled, the subject line and attachments follow naturally. Filled in first, they often no longer match what the letter says.

## One Letter, One Purpose

Do not combine a funding request, an invitation, and a report in one letter. The recipient will not know which one to answer. Three short, clear letters beat one long, confusing one.

## Mistakes That Keep Coming Back

From experience, these show up again and again:

1. Casual street language slips into a formal letter.
2. The first paragraph does not make the point clear.
3. The letterhead or signature is incomplete.
4. The letter mentions an attachment that was never included.
5. It says "immediately" with no clear deadline.
6. Names or terms are spelled inconsistently.

Number five is easy to miss. "Immediately" may mean tomorrow to the sender and next month to the receiver. Write the date.

## Checking Before You Send

Read the letter out loud. Your ears catch awkward sentences that your eyes skip. Check the date and the recipient's name, make sure the attachment is really there, and ask one other person to read it.

If you write many letters each week, keep a finished letter as a template so the same mistakes do not come back.

The AI agent your team uses can help too. It can prepare drafts, tidy the language, and check spelling or inconsistent names. But people decide the content, and people sign. A finished letter should be read once more by the person responsible before it goes out.

## It Comes Down to Trust

A tidy letter is not just formality. It is about trust. An organization whose letters are tidy is easier to trust, and its business moves along more smoothly.
