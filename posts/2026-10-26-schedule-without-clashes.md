Picture a Sunday morning. A regular study session starts at eight, and at the exact same hour there is a community clean-up that was announced two weeks ago. The same person is expected at both.

Three people ask at once, "So who has to give way?" Nobody knows, because nobody did anything wrong. What was missing was a way of building the schedule.

## Start from one calendar, not five

Organization schedules are usually scattered. Some sit in group chats, some in a board member's notes, some on a notice board, some in the chairperson's memory. Clashes are born here, because no single place shows everything at once.

Gather every schedule in one spot. If there is no shared digital calendar yet, a big sheet of paper on the wall works fine. What matters is that everyone looks at the same source.

## Three sources of clashes

In practice, clashes almost always come from three things.

- Forgetting that one person sits on two committees or two boards.
- Not counting travel time or the time needed to move between places.
- Fixing the date before the venue is clear.

The first one is the most common. We schedule a meeting calmly, while half the attendees already have something else at that hour.

The second is subtler. Two events do not overlap on the calendar, but one ends exactly when the other begins somewhere else.

## A buffer rule and a key holder

A simple rule of thumb: leave at least 30 minutes between two events held at different places. If the distance is long, add more.

Then name one person as the calendar keeper. Not to decide everything, but so there is a single door. Anyone who wants to add an event proposes it through that person, who checks whether the slot is actually free.

Without a keeper, everyone feels responsible for the schedule, and in the end nobody truly looks after it.

## Helped by automation, with people still deciding

Recurring activities such as study sessions, meetings, and weekly events should be entered once in the shared calendar. After that, nobody retypes them every week.

This is where an AI agent can help. It can draft a weekly summary from the calendar, then remind the board through WhatsApp, Telegram, or email. The dull, easy-to-miss work gets lighter.

But language models can be wrong. One might misread a time or skip an entry. So a person should read the output before it goes out to everyone. The machine helps tidy up, and people make the call.

## Wrapping up

The most useful habit to start this week is tiny: set aside 15 minutes every week, open the calendar, and check the week ahead. Look for overlaps, look for events with no clear venue, and sort them out before announcing anything.

Those fifteen minutes usually save hours of apologizing later.
