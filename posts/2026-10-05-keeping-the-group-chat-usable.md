Every group chat ends up telling the same story: hundreds of unread messages, and the one you
actually need is nowhere. Not because nothing important was said — because too many important
things were said in the same place.

The problem is not the number of messages. The problem is that nobody knows where a piece of
information lives, or who is supposed to keep it.

## Split by function

One channel for announcements: only the committee posts, everyone else reads. One channel for
questions: open to all, but each question gets a few lines, not an essay. One more for the
technical detail that only concerns a small team.

Mix all three and announcements will always lose the race against conversation. Someone who
just wants the schedule has to dig it out from between jokes and thank-you notes.

## One message, one topic

A message that stacks three questions into a single paragraph will, almost certainly, get one
answer. Split it. Question on the first line, context underneath. People read the group in the
gaps between other work, not at a desk with a notebook open.

## Set quiet hours

Big decisions do not get made at midnight. A healthy organisation does not demand an answer
within five minutes. If something really is urgent, call — that is a different channel, and
that is exactly what it is for.

## Someone has to write the summary

At the end of each week, one person writes a short summary: what was decided, what was
postponed, which files matter. That summary gets pinned to the top instead of drifting away in
the middle of the thread.

This habit is the most underrated of the lot, and it saves the most time. Next week, the same
question does not need to be asked again.

To make the summary useful, write decisions in the words other people will remember: the name
of the event, the date, who is responsible. Not "as we discussed yesterday".

## Three replies in, make a call

A thread that is still unresolved after three replies is usually not short of information. It
is simply a harder problem. Move it to a short call, then bring the result back to the group
as a single paragraph of decision.

## Where an assistant earns its keep

An assistant wired into a chat channel is most useful for three things: summarising long
threads, recording decisions, and reminding people of dates. But no assistant fixes a habit
that was never there. A messy group stays messy unless someone is genuinely responsible for
tidying it. The assistant only makes that job lighter.

> A tidy group is not the quietest one. A tidy group is one whose contents can still be found
> when you need them.

Here is a simple test: leave the group alone for a week, then try to find one old decision. If
it takes you under two minutes, your group works. If it does not, what you need is not a new
group — it is a shared rule that people actually follow.
