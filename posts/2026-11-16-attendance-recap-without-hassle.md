The smallest sheet of paper in the event folder is usually the one people ask for most. The attendance list. A few months after an event, somebody wants to know how many people came to that study circle. Three people start digging, one tries to remember, and the answer lands on the word "around".

## Late is rarely about laziness

A recap that arrives late seldom comes from laziness. It comes from a sequence that runs backwards. The sheet gets passed around as the event is breaking up: people are standing, holding a cup, saying goodbye, and the next signature is unreadable. The paper goes into a bag, and the recap gets written a month later from memory. By then the work is no longer recording. It is guessing.

## Collect at the door, not the exit

Move the signing to the start. One person stands near the entrance and hands the book to whoever walks in. The queue takes five seconds, and the name gets written while the person still remembers which event they came for.

Make the sheet easy before the event begins. The names of the core committee are pre-printed, so they only need a tick. Blank rows are left for guests. Decide the columns in advance: date, event, name, note. A sheet that already looks like a recap makes the typing later much faster.

## One place, one naming rule

Data rarely disappears. It spreads: a photo on someone's phone, a book in a cupboard, a note in the WhatsApp group, one file on a laptop nobody claims. Pick one folder per period and stay in it.

The habit that helps most is plain. Photograph the sheet right after the event and save it with a clear name, date first, then the event. A good filename is already an archive. Six months later nobody opens twenty photos. They search one name.

## Automation, with a human check

Some technology can cut the rest of the work. A scheduled reminder every Saturday on WhatsApp or Telegram with one question: has this week's attendance sheet been uploaded? A bot can read the photo and add rows to the table. A language model can turn messy notes from a voice message into a table people can read. Connect the agent to the folder and the spreadsheet through MCP and the copy-pasting stops.

The line to hold: machines are good at moving and tidying, poor at confirming. Names and counts that end up in a report still get checked by a person. One misspelled name costs more to fix than ten minutes of copying.

## A recap people actually read

A useful recap fits on one page. How many events this month, the average turnout, which event dropped, what comes next. Numbers without a sentence of meaning are just a table people skip. A single line, say "the evening study circle dropped because it clashed with the neighbourhood meeting", makes the numbers speak.

Keep the raw list as an appendix. The first page is what the chairman and the donors read.

No new app to learn. Two things change: when the sheet gets filled in, and where the paper goes after the event. Those two small things separate a recap finished in five minutes from one postponed until it is forgotten. 📋
