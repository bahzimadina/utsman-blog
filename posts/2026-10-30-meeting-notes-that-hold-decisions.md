## Everyone Goes Home with a Different Memory

The meeting breaks up, chairs get pushed back, and everybody leaves. A week later, three people are sure the decision was A, one is sure it was B, and one has just realized they were handed a task.

The cause is often simple. Our minutes carefully record who spoke and what was discussed, but forget to record what was decided. The result is a long document nobody wants to read again.

## Discussion Topics, Proposals, Decisions

Three kinds of talk get mixed together in a meeting. A discussion topic is the subject being talked about. A proposal is an idea someone throws out. A decision is a proposal that the group finally agreed to carry out.

A useful summary does not need to copy all three in equal measure. Just highlight one: the decision. Topics and proposals can be shortened, or dropped if they changed nothing.

## The Decision Box

Put a small box at the very top that can be read in ten seconds. It holds four things:

- Decided: what will be done
- Owner: who carries it out
- Starts: when it begins
- Watch: what to check to know it is running

The rest can be plain paragraphs. Add a short bit of context on why the choice was made, so anyone who asks "why did we do that?" gets an answer right away.

## A Decision Sentence You Can Test

A good decision sentence has a verb, a result, and a deadline. Compare them. A bad example: "On the schedule for the regular study session, we agreed to consider it again." That decides nothing, and nobody can check it.

A good example: "The regular study session moves to Sunday morning starting next month." There is the verb (moves), the result (Sunday morning), and the deadline (next month). Even someone who missed the meeting can tell whether it has happened or not.

## Be Honest About What Is Undecided

There is always something left hanging, and that is normal. The trouble starts when we pretend everything is settled. Give it its own section: what is undecided, who holds it, and when it will be discussed again.

For example: "The venue is not decided. The secretary will check two options, then we revisit it at next week's meeting." This honesty actually prevents repeat meetings, because everyone knows exactly what is still open and nobody reopens what is already closed.

## Send It Fast, Then Test It

Send the summary the same day, one page at most, through WhatsApp, Telegram, or email. The longer you wait, the blurrier everyone's memory gets, and the more stubborn each person's own version becomes.

An AI agent or language model can help tidy a transcript into a draft, or schedule follow-up reminders automatically. But a human still checks the decisions. AI must never invent a decision that nobody said in the meeting.

The test is simple: hand the summary to someone who was not there. If they understand what to do tomorrow, your summary worked.
