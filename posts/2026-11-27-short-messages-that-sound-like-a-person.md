## The message people skip

You send an important announcement. Two people reply. Often the reason is small: it sounds like a machine.

"With regard to the upcoming activity, the presence of all members is expected." The sentence is correct. It is polite. Then it lands in a WhatsApp group and people scroll straight past it. Not because the content does not matter, but because reading it feels like processing an official letter.

Messages that work tend to be short, clear, and have a person inside them.

## Why a message feels robotic

First, the opening is stacked: three lines of throat-clearing before the actual point lands in line six. People read between tasks. They have no patience for that.

Second, everything in it is abstract: activity, participation, support, smooth running. Nothing there can be pictured.

Third, no details stick. What time, which room, what to bring, who to ask. Without details people have to ask, and most would rather stay quiet.

## One message, one purpose

Before writing, decide one thing: what should the reader do next? Come, fill a form, bring something, or simply know. Two purposes means two messages.

Then order it the way a person talks: what is happening, when, where, who to ask. Done. No long opener, no flowery closing.

An example:

> Regular study circle tonight after Maghrib, in the mosque hall. Topic: how to treat your neighbours. If you are coming, tell the committee so we can set out enough chairs.

Compare it with the usual version: "All congregants are hereby informed that a regular study circle will be held." The first puts chairs, a time, and a human contact in your head. The second puts a document there.

## Swap paper words for mouth words

Some words only live in documents: utilise, herewith, aforementioned, pursuant to. All can be replaced without losing politeness.

"Utilise" becomes "use". "Pursuant to" becomes "about" or "because". "Attendance is expected" becomes "please come". "If it is agreeable" becomes "if you can".

Politeness is not about long words. It is about respecting the reader's time.

## Tools can help, but do not erase the human

Language models can tidy a sentence in seconds. Write a messy draft, ask for a cleanup, read it again. The trouble is that the tidiest version often wipes out what made it ours: the local way of greeting people, a detail only the committee knows, a warm tone no machine can copy.

So use the tool in the middle, not at the end. Let it fix the structure, then add one human line yourself: "Chairs are limited, forty of them. If you usually bring your own prayer mat, bring it again."

Automation earns its keep on the boring parts: reminders, announcements re-sent to a WhatsApp or Telegram channel, recording who has confirmed. The final wording still belongs to a person, and one person should read it again before it goes out to a crowd.

## The ten second test

Before sending, read it out loud. If you run out of breath mid-sentence, the sentence is too long. If some part makes you reluctant to read it yourself, cut it.

Send it to yourself first, open it on a phone, and check whether the point is visible without scrolling. If it is not, put the point in the first line.

A good short message does not have to be the tidiest. It has to be understood fast, say what happens next, and feel like a person wrote it. That is what gets replies, not just reads.
