Behind every new tool introduced to an organization lies a long story of people trying to learn it. The problem is rarely the tool itself. More often, the problem is how it is taught. Training sessions often turn into sleep fests because the presenter gets lost in features while the audience wonders where to even begin.

I remember when our team decided to switch to a digital system for daily activity schedules. Everyone agreed at first. But once the projector lit up and foreign terms like "automation", "MCP", and "bot reminders" started appearing, the faces in the room changed. Some looked down at their phones. Others pretended to take notes with blank stares. The session ended without anyone truly understanding.

From that moment, we learned that the way we teach had to change. First, never dump every feature in one sitting. The human brain has limits. We broke the material into tiny pieces that anyone could finish in ten minutes. Today, just practice creating a schedule. Tomorrow, learn how to edit it. Slow and steady, but it sticks.

Second, use examples straight from the workplace. Avoid random internet scenarios that feel distant. We used our weekly study schedule, previously written in a notebook, and showed how the new tool could send automatic reminders via short messages. Someone immediately said, "Oh, so it can do that?" That moment carried more weight than any long speech.

Third, let people practice hands-on. Do not make them watch. We prepared spare laptops and asked members to try it themselves. Made a mistake? No problem. We deliberately kept the mood light when someone clicked the wrong button. That is exactly how memory forms.

Fourth, keep the pressure low. We turned Q&A sessions into casual chats on the mosque terrace over coffee in the late afternoon. The relaxed setting made even silly questions feel completely safe to ask.

Fifth, celebrate every small effort. When a member sent their first test message through the bot, we highlighted it in the group chat. Not to show off, but to build real confidence.

Now, almost every member actively uses the new tool without needing constant reminders. Change takes time. The key is to start with small steps and make sure no one gets left behind.
