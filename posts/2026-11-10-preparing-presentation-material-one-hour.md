There is a meeting at nine, and at eight someone says: "You're presenting at noon, right?" One hour. Panic is fair, but time is rarely what's missing. What eats the hour is the wish to build a beautiful deck.

One hour is enough if the order is right. Most people open the app first, pick a template, change the font, and an hour later there is still no content. Flip it. Think first, open the app last.

## One message first, not ten

Before touching anything, write one sentence. If the audience is allowed to remember one thing from this talk, what is it? One sentence only, sayable without reading notes.

Once that sentence exists, everything else is easy to judge. A slide that doesn't support it goes. A number that doesn't clarify it goes. People prepare ten ideas when what's needed is one, repeated in different ways.

## An outline on paper, not on slides

Take a sheet of paper and write the flow:

- The situation as it stands today.
- What has already been done.
- The result, with numbers you actually know.
- What you're asking the audience for: a decision, support, or hands.

That covers most talks. For each part, decide on one slide. A ten-minute talk needs seven to nine slides, not thirty.

## Then open the app

With the outline in place, the app becomes a tool, not a place to think. Use a template you've used before. Don't shop for fonts, don't hunt for illustrations, don't play with colour. All of that can wait.

One slide, one idea. Five lines of text at most, large. Replace tables with one big number and one sentence explaining it. People read slides while listening, and can't do both when a slide is packed.

## Write what you'll actually say

The part most often skipped: the spoken script. Write three sentences in the speaker notes for each slide: the opening line, the core, the bridge to the next one. This is what will really come out of your mouth.

Then rehearse once, standing, with a timer. Once only. Don't repeat until memorised, it sounds stiff. If you run over, cut a slide instead of talking faster.

## What makes it fast: material you already have

A one-hour presentation usually reuses old material. Event photos sit in the WhatsApp group, last month's report in a folder, attendance data in a spreadsheet. Gather it. Don't start from blank.

Language tools speed this up too. Paste messy notes, ask for an outline or a five-line summary. The catch: the output still needs checking. Those tools are good at cutting sentences, not at inventing numbers. Numbers still have to come from your own notes.

What helps over the long run is the small stuff that runs by itself: one template folder, one checklist, a scheduled reminder a few days before the event. Next meeting, the same preparation takes twenty minutes. 🕐

## Close calmly

If ten minutes are left and the slides are still rough, close the app. Take four points on paper, then speak calmly. A clear talk with simple slides lands better than a beautiful deck that isn't finished and gets delivered in a panic.
