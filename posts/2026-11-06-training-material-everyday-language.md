The training runs for two hours. Participants sit politely, take a few notes, nod at the right moments. At the end they go home with a photocopied packet nearly as thick as a finger. A week later, ask what was in it. Hardly anyone remembers.

It's easy to blame the participants: not focused, slow to catch on. But the cause is usually on the page itself. The material was written like an office document, not like a person explaining something.

## The language is document language, not people language

Try reading one page of training material with a participant's ears. The sentences sprawl: one sentence carries three clauses and a pair of parentheses. Foreign terms pile up, abbreviations appear with no explanation, as if everyone had memorized what they stand for. Participants usually stop at the second sentence. Not out of laziness, but because there's nothing to hold on to.

## Write as if explaining to one person

Picture just one participant, the busiest one in the room. Little time, a desk full of work. Write the sentences you would actually say to them if you were sitting across the table.

Compare two sentences. First: "Incomplete documentation results in processing delays." Second: "If this field is empty, the file comes back next week." Same content. But the second one is easy to picture, and the reader knows what to do tomorrow morning.

## One page, one thought

Break the material into small pieces. Each page holds one thing the participant must remember, no more. Two ideas means two pages. It sounds wasteful with paper, but people always know where they are, and they can find the part they need again.

## Examples from the participants' own desks

Made-up examples feel far from real life. Use real cases: incoming letters piling up, schedules that clash, attendance sheets that are never complete. Even better, ask participants to name their own examples. That way the material becomes theirs, not just the trainer's notes.

## Test it two ways

First, read it aloud. If you stumble mid-sentence, the participants will too, so fix it. Second, ask one person to explain the page back in their own words. If the explanation misses, it isn't the participant's fault. The material isn't clear yet.

## Simple doesn't mean shallow

Everyday language doesn't mean throwing out technical terms. They're still welcome, as long as each one is explained once, early, in a short sentence, and then used consistently to the end. Analogies help too, up to a point. Once an analogy needs an explanation of its own, drop it and go back to a real example.

Good material feels like being chatted with by someone who understands our work. No talking down, no showing off vocabulary. The last test is simple: give it to someone who didn't attend the training and ask what they took from it. The answer will be honest, and it tells you whether the material is doing its job.
