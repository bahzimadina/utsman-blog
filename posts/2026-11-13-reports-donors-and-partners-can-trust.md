The twenty-page report was finished. Neat, complete, a photo in every section. It went out by email and came back as one line: "Thanks, so which programme worked best?" A simple question, and the exact place where the report failed. It described what we did, not what changed.

## Your reader is not a boss

Reports to donors get written like reports to a manager: proof that we have been busy. A donor is not a manager. They are someone who already placed trust with you, deciding whether to renew it next year. A partner sits in the same seat, working out whether the arrangement still makes sense.

Once you see who is reading, half the sections that felt mandatory start to look excessive. The committee list, the opening remarks, the full attendance sheet. Donors rarely ask for those. They ask whether the thing you promised arrived.

## Start with what changed

"We held twelve meetings" is activity. "Eleven of the fifteen people who came in the first month were still coming three months later" is change. The second one takes more work to record, because the number is not sitting in the minutes. It is also the one that gets read.

When change is hard to measure, one short story does the job. Not a sentimental one, but one with a before and an after.

## Three parts, one page

Shape it simply: what changed, what did not go to plan, what comes next. Close with one clear ask if there is one. Order matters. Do not bury the second part on page nineteen.

One page forces choices. That is not a flaw. Thick reports become the place where every unresolved thing is stored, and the reader carries it.

## A few numbers, with something to compare

Three numbers with a comparison beat twenty bare ones. "Forty people" means little without "out of sixty invited" or "up from twenty-five last month". When there is no number, one sentence from somebody who benefited beats an empty table.

Be careful with numbers that look too good. A figure rounded upward always shows up in the next report.

## Tools collect, people decide

Gathering the material is usually the tiring part. Notes sit in a WhatsApp group, in email, in Telegram threads; photos live on committee members' phones. An AI agent can sweep those channels, pull everything into one draft and put events in order. A language model can cut a long draft to a page, or turn messy notes into readable paragraphs.

What stays with a person: choosing which numbers are honest and which merely look good. That needs someone who knows the context.

## Honesty buys credibility

A short section on what fell short, the quiet session, the target not reached, makes the rest easier to believe. Anyone who has run a programme knows nothing lands at a hundred percent. A report that admits something is more convincing than one that is too smooth.

## Keep a rhythm

Do not vanish for eleven months and reappear with a thick report. A short note every month, three paragraphs, one photo, one number, holds a relationship better than one big document at once. The full report still matters, usually each quarter or at year end.

## The one-paragraph test

Before sending, close the file and read only the opening paragraph. If a reader cannot tell what changed and who felt it, rewrite it. The rest can be tidied afterwards.
