Ever catch yourself hunting for a save shortcut without touching the mouse? Or drawing a blank on a terminal command you just used yesterday? It is maddening. The tool is open almost every day, yet your brain files the know-how in some dusty corner.

I used to do the same. I would find a cool shortcut in a video, try it twice, then forget it by morning. Back to the old routine: click here, scroll there, hunt around. All for a task that takes one second with the right key combo.

The fix is simple: keep a tiny personal note. Not a thick manual — just a single page or text file listing the commands you use most. People call it a cheat sheet. Think of it like a shopping list: short, practical, straight to the point.

Making one is easy. Pick the tool you use daily. Jot down five to ten commands you look up repeatedly. In a terminal, that might be quick folder jumps, file searches, or recursive copies. In a text editor, note shortcuts for moving lines, duplicating, or find-and-replace. In chat or email apps, write down text formatting or silent-send tricks.

Format is up to you. A two-column table, a bullet list, a sticky note on your monitor — whatever reads fastest in a glance. Do not polish it so much that finding an entry becomes its own chore. That defeats the purpose.

If you stare at a screen all day, try keeping the cheat sheet as your desktop wallpaper or as a tiny text file parked in a screen corner. Some folks tape a paper card beside their monitor. Use whatever feels natural.

Tech moves fast. New helpers like AI agents, large language models, and automation systems appear every year. Each has its own commands and usage patterns. Training yourself to write down only the essentials beats trying to memorize everything.

A cheat sheet is not a sign of a bad memory. It is a sign that you respect your limits and want to work smarter. Start with just one tool today. Add another next week. Over time, both your desk and your head feel clearer.
