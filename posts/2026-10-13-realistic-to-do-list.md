Most of us have written a twenty-line to-do list in the morning and crossed off three items by night. It feels like failure. But usually the person didn't fail. The list did.

A bad to-do list isn't a plan. It's a place to dump anxiety. Everything floating around in your head goes onto paper, with no thought about how long each item will take.

## What an unrealistic list looks like

First, it's too long. A working day rarely holds more than five or six hours of real focus. The rest disappears into messages, small meetings, and surprises.

Second, the tasks are too big and too vague. "Handle the activity report" isn't a task, it's a project. Our brains tend to put off anything when it's unclear where to begin.

Third, there's no slack. A list packed from morning to evening collapses the moment one thing slips.

## Building one that makes sense

Start with a simple question: if only three things get done today, which three make the day worthwhile? Write those first. Everything else can stay on the page, but it counts as a bonus.

Then break big tasks into steps that take half an hour or less. "Draft the invitation" is far lighter than "prepare the event".

Estimate the time, then multiply by about one and a half. We are almost always too optimistic about duration. This one habit saves a lot of afternoons.

> A good list tells you when you're allowed to stop.

Finally, keep roughly a third of the day free for the unplanned. It will show up anyway, so you may as well leave it a seat.

## A word about technology

Some people on our team use AI agents to help with this. For example, an agent that reads incoming WhatsApp or email messages and summarizes what needs a follow-up, or one that sends scheduled reminders at set times. It's useful, within limits.

Tools like that are good at sorting and nudging. They can't decide what matters most to you. If the list is already bloated, automation just makes the mess move faster. Sort out the thinking first, then hand the boring parts to the machine.

## An end-of-day habit

Before you stop working, take five minutes. Look at what got done and what didn't, then move the leftovers to tomorrow honestly. If a task has been moved three times, it's either not important or too big and needs splitting. Delete it or break it up.

Crossing something off feels good, and that feeling is worth protecting. A short list that gets finished beats a long one that haunts you. Tomorrow, try writing just three things and see how the day goes.
