You send a notice about the Saturday cleanup, date and time included. Ten minutes later someone asks: so what day was that again? That is not a sign that people read carelessly. More often it means the notice never did its job.

A clear announcement is not about elegant wording. One thing matters: after reading it, people know what to do next. If readers have to draw their own conclusion, they will draw different ones, and the organizer ends up answering the same question again and again.

## The real problem is buried information

Notices are often written like a story. A long opening, background first, and the actual point somewhere in the third paragraph. People skim on a phone screen, so anything sitting at the bottom gets skipped.

Another habit is writing as if readers already know the context. Internal shorthand, an event name nobody explained, a room referred to by a nickname. Clear to the committee, confusing to everyone else.

## Put the point up front

The safest order is this: what the event is, when, where, who should come, then the extra notes. For a cleanup invitation, the first sentence can carry four of those at once.

> Example: "Mosque cleanup this Saturday, seven in the morning, meet at the front yard. Bring your own cleaning tools."

Two lines, and every basic question is answered. Short sentences also help people who read while walking or on a small screen. One message, one line. If there are three separate things, make three lines.

## One ask, not three

Many notices stack requests at once: come, bring this, confirm, then contact the committee. When everything is urgent, nothing feels urgent. Pick the single action you care about most, put it at the end, and keep it plain. Confirm with your neighbourhood coordinator, or simply show up on time.

## Test it before you send

There is a cheap way to test a notice. Ask one person who was not in the planning meeting to read it, then have them repeat it back. Whatever they get wrong is the part that needs fixing.

Mind the channel too. A message in a group chat should be short and stand on its own, with no attachment required. A poster needs fewer words and bigger type. Both can come from one draft, cut in different ways.

## Help from an agent, still checked by a person

Here at the mosque, draft notices are sometimes prepared with help from an AI agent, a program that reads the meeting notes and puts together a first version. A person still reviews it before it goes out, because the agent does not know who already heard the news by word of mouth and who has not.

The agent is useful for checking the draft itself, too. Automated checks flag tangled sentences and details that should not be shared publicly. Clarity, though, is easiest to test with a human: if the reader asks a question back, something is still missing.

A good announcement feels boring to the person who wrote it, because nothing is left open to interpretation. That is exactly the sign it works. The job is simple: let people understand without having to call the committee first.
