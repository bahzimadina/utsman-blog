One of the most nerve-wracking moments in event organizing is when a team member suddenly says they cannot make it. That is not a big deal if you have prepared. But if you have not, a small hiccup can ripple through the entire program.

The real problem is usually not the missing body; it is the missing handover. The person who cannot come often carries critical information in their head: who to call, what supplies are on the way, or which permits are still pending. When that information walks out the door with them, the people on the ground lose direction.

The simplest way to avoid this is to build a clear task list well ahead of time. Every role — from field coordinator to catering lead, documentation team, and cleanup crew — should have a backup name assigned. Not just any name, but someone who has agreed and been briefed on the scenario.

Beyond that, each administrator should keep a short set of notes that can be handed over. This can be a piece of paper, a note on a phone, or a quick message in a group chat. The point is: if they wake up sick tomorrow, someone else can read those notes without guessing.

When passing the baton, skip the long speech. Just mention three things: what needs to be done, who the key contacts are, and the deadlines that must be met. The rest can be asked on the spot. The replacement also needs room to ask questions, not just absorb orders.

At the mosque where we serve, we have started a habit of holding a short meeting one week before any major event. There, every administrator names their substitute and highlights any special concerns. Over time this has become part of our culture. Recently one of our team members came down with a fever three days before a large religious gathering. Because a replacement was already prepared, the event went ahead without anyone feeling lost.

Technology can help, but it does not have to be fancy. A group messaging app is enough to share quick updates. If you want something neater, a simple shared spreadsheet works well too. What matters is not the tool, but the habit of always having a fallback plan.

During a packed weekend, illness or family matters can hit anyone. What separates a well-run organization from a chaotic one is whether they thought about that possibility beforehand.
