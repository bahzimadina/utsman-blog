Plenty of people can explain their work neatly in writing, then lose the words the moment they have to stand up and speak. It isn't a lack of understanding. The practice just keeps getting postponed until the event is nearly here.

Practice doesn't need a course, a special room, or two hours. Five to ten minutes a day is enough, as long as it's repeated and done the right way.

## Start with one sentence

Before working on delivery, decide on the one sentence you want people to remember. If the audience gets to take home one thing, what is it? Write that sentence, then say it out loud five times in different tones: flat, firm, relaxed.

It becomes clear fast which one sounds best. A sentence that's easy to say usually lands well on other ears too.

## Shrink the audience

Nerves come from a head already picturing a hundred pairs of eyes. So shrink the number first. Talk to one person: a colleague, your spouse, or the phone camera. Afterwards ask one thing only, "What do you remember?"

Their answer is more useful than your own judgement. If what they remember isn't the point you prepared, the order still needs work.

## Record, don't grade

Your phone is enough of a training tool. Record two minutes, then listen once without commenting on anything. After that, note only three things: filler words that keep slipping out, a pace that leaves you out of breath, and whether your eyes stay glued to the notes.

Don't fix ten things at once. One fix per recording is fine. In a week, three small habits can disappear.

## Drills you can slot into the day

No special time needed. Some of these fit between other tasks:

- Read one paragraph aloud while standing, two minutes only.
- Answer one question with no notes for a minute, something like "why does this work matter?"
- Pause two seconds after a key point instead of filling the gap with "umm".
- Record ninety seconds, listen back, write one thing to fix tomorrow.

## Tools help, they don't practise for you

Voice notes can be turned into text, then asked to be shaped into an outline or a short summary. Language tools are also decent at flagging sentences that go round in circles. What matters: check the result yourself, and remember a tool can't hand you calm. Calm comes from repetition, not from a perfect script.

One more small thing that helps: a scheduled reminder for ten minutes of practice, and a short checklist before you go up. Once it's routine, preparation feels light.

## The goal isn't a perfect speech

The aim here is simple: people understand you. Not looking brilliant, not never fumbling a word. Even the calmest speakers get stuck sometimes.

Start in small rooms, like a three-minute update at a meeting. After a while the body gets used to it, and the nerves shrink on their own. 🎤
