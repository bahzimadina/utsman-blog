Every time an event ends, the same question lands in the committee group chat: is the report done yet? Then silence. Not because nobody wants to write it, but because a report sounds like a thick document full of tables that eats a week.

The reports that actually get read tend to be one page long. Board members read them between meetings, on the way, or waiting for the call to prayer. What they want is not a complete document but quick answers: did the event work, what came out of it, and what needs fixing next.

## What has to be there

One page holds plenty if you write with discipline. Five things need to be in it:

- The name of the event, the date, and the place, stated plainly.
- How many people came, without listing names one by one.
- The run of the programme in three to five lines, not a minute-by-minute schedule.
- Visible results: what changed, what got delayed, what failed.
- A short note on problems, plus a suggestion for next time.

The rest can go. The rundown sits in the proposal, the attendance sheet in its own file. What belongs in the report are things no other document captures: how the room felt, how residents reacted, and the surprises of the day.

## Write it the day after

The best time to write is not a week later but the day after. Memory is still intact, room names are still clear, and the tiredness has not worn off, so small problems get written down too. Wait too long and only the outline is left, and the report turns tidy and empty.

Raw material usually sits in the committee's WhatsApp or Telegram thread: photos, meeting notes, complaints about the sound system, a shortage of food. Gather it into one note, then clean it up. A rough draft written that day beats a polished report that never gets finished.

## One page forces choices

The limit feels cruel at first, then becomes an exercise in thinking: what matters, and what was only interesting to you. Committee members who worked hard deserve a mention; the technical details of putting up the tent do not.

Numbers too. How many attended, how many sessions ran, how many invitations went out: all useful. What does not need writing down is internal detail that shifts quickly, because a report that circulates widely should still make sense months later.

## Tools help, they do not think for you

Language tools are decent at turning messy notes into readable paragraphs. Paste in the scruffy minutes, ask for a one-page summary, then check the facts. They are good at cutting rambling sentences and reordering points, not at adding events that never happened.

Scheduled reminders help more. Set it so a task to write the report appears on its own the day after every event. Reports pile up not from laziness but because nobody nudges at the right moment.

## Start from a template

Keep one template file with those five sections. Next time you fill in the blanks, and writing drops from two hours to twenty minutes. Write it plainly first, tidy it after. A one-page report finished today beats a perfect one still sitting in your head. 📝
