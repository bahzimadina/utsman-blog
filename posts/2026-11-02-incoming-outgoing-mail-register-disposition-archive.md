A letter arrives at nine, gets skimmed, and lands on the desk. Two weeks later someone asks about
it and nobody remembers where the paper went. Not laziness. Usually it is because there is no
single place that answers the simplest question of all: whose hands is this letter in right
now? 📥

## The register is not paperwork theatre

One line per letter. Keep the columns few: sequence number, date received, sender, a short
subject, where it was passed on, and the date it closed. Too many columns and nobody bothers to
fill them in, and the book ends up blank.

The register does not store the letter's contents. It answers one thing: where is this letter
now. A half-filled register is still far more useful than a thick, tidy book nobody ever opens.

## A disposition should not stop at the first desk

"Please follow up" is the sentence that most often ends with nothing happening. A slip that works
carries three things: what has to be done, who does it, and by when. When it is done, the letter
goes back to whoever keeps the register, with a note on the outcome. It does not sit on the first
desk waiting for a miracle.

## One naming pattern for the archive

A file saved as `letter-help-new.pdf` will be lost within three months. A pattern that survives is
simple: date first, then a short description.

- `2026-11-02-invitation-letter-meeting.pdf`
- `2026-11-02-reply-to-invitation.pdf`

One folder per year, and finished letters kept apart from the ones still in play. That keeps the
active list short, and nobody has to open files one by one to find something.

## Outgoing mail needs a number too

Outgoing mail tends to be treated as finished the moment it is sent. That is exactly where the
trouble shows up: duplicate reference numbers, or no copy to produce when someone asks. One
running number per year, one digital copy stored with the same naming pattern as incoming mail,
and the problem is gone. The tool matters far less than the habit of saving the copy on the day
the letter goes out.

## A small rhythm at the end of the day

Ten minutes before leaving is usually enough. Today's mail gets recorded today, and whatever is
still open gets a look once a week. Without that rhythm the register slowly turns into an empty
building: it exists, but nobody lives there.

## The part you can hand over

The tiring part is not deciding, it is copying. Photograph a letter into a WhatsApp or Telegram
channel wired to an agent, and the agent writes it into the register, summarises the contents,
and suggests who should receive it. A scheduled reminder can flag letters that blew past their
deadline without anyone having to keep it in their head.

All of that is a suggestion, not a decision. What stays with the committee: judging whether a
letter matters, and signing it. Those two should not be automated, because that is where the
responsibility sits.

A tidy organisation is not the one with the most tools. It is the one that answers two questions
fastest: where is this letter, and who is holding it.
