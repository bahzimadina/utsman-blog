Every morning I open my inbox. The unread count is already in the thousands. I look at it for a second, my stomach tightens a bit, and I close it again.

That guilt solves nothing. The next morning I do the same thing. Eventually I noticed the problem wasn't laziness. I was treating my inbox like a warehouse, when it should be an intake desk.

## Why it piles up

There are three causes, and I did all three myself. First, I used email as storage. Invitations, receipts, committee announcements, all left sitting there. Second, notifications barked every minute, so my attention kept getting cut. Third, I assumed every message had to be read right away.

Most of them didn't need me at all.

## One big sweep

The first step that worked for me: set aside a block of 60 to 90 minutes, close everything else, and clear it in one go. Don't do it in small pieces.

Sort by sender, not by date. That way one sender who mailed fifty newsletters can be dealt with in a single click. Then I sort what's left into three baskets:

- reply now
- needs action, goes on the task list
- archive or delete

Anything unclear goes into one folder called "later". It doesn't stay in the inbox. I open that folder once a week, and oddly enough half of it has stopped mattering by then.

## Three questions per email

Once it's clean, I hold to a simple rule. For each email I ask: is this my business? Does it need action? If so, when?

If it needs no action and I won't need to read it again, I archive it without hesitation. I used to be afraid of deleting. Now I remember that email search is good enough to find whatever I might need later.

## Change the habit

I turned off every email notification. I check twice a day, say after the dawn prayer and again in the late afternoon. Outside those windows, nothing.

There's one rule I keep strictly: no email stays overnight in the inbox for more than one working day. Either I reply, schedule it, or archive it.

## Filters and labels

System notifications, bills, and newsletters go straight to their own folders through filters. The inbox holds only messages from real people who are waiting for an answer. Once this runs, the number on screen becomes reasonable and stops being frightening.

## Help from an AI agent

Lately an AI agent helps me through channels I already use, WhatsApp and Telegram. It summarizes long emails, drafts replies, groups messages by topic, and reminds me of ones I haven't answered.

The limits are clear, though. I read every draft before it goes out. For sensitive matters there is no automatic sending. And I always check the agent's work, because a tidy summary isn't necessarily a correct one.

## What I'm really after

The goal isn't an empty inbox. That's just a bonus. What I want is to know what is waiting for me, and to be sure nothing important has quietly slipped away.
